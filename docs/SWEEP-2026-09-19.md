## F-A · NOT FIXED · The phone chooses its own operator, and the database believes it

**What happens today.** `src/backend/dispatch.ts:147` downloads the *entire* `operators`
collection to the traveler's phone. The phone runs the match itself, then writes
`rides/{id}` with the operator it picked (`dispatch.ts:253–262`). `firestore.rules` permits
that create with `allow create: if ownsIncoming()`, which checks one thing: that
`travelerUid` is the signed-in user. **Nothing validates the operator assignment.**

**Three consequences, in order of seriousness.**

**1. The safety gates do not apply to the main booking path.** `matchOperator` in
`backend/matching.js` refuses an operator who is off duty, stale, screening-blocked, out of
insurance, or on an old statutory disclosure. Those gates run in `monitor.js` and
`scheduler.js` — the re-offer sweep and scheduled travel. The ordinary booking a traveler
makes does not pass through any of them. A crafted client can assign a travel to an operator
who is off duty, whose commercial cover has lapsed, whose background check was refused, or
who never read the §627.748(8)(a) disclosure. The disclosure gate added today (`08c0d89`)
hardens the chokepoint; this path does not go through the chokepoint.

**2. Every operator's live position is readable by every traveler.** The fleet document holds
`lat`, `lng`, `onlineAt`, `name`, `car`, `plate`, `insuranceExpiry`, `screened`,
`screeningCheckedAt`. Any signed-in account can read all of it for every operator,
continuously. Operators are private individuals, frequently working alone at night. This is a
tracking capability, and it is also their insurance and background-check status.

**3. It does not scale, and it is billed per read.** Every dispatch attempt reads every
operator document. At 5,000 operators and 10,000 travels a day that is 50 million document
reads a day, for a job the server can do with one query.

**What is NOT wrong.** The money is safe. `/create-payment-intent` prices on the server and
says so — "Never from a client-sent amount" — and `transferToOperator` calculates the
operator's share from Stripe's own metadata on the payment, not from the travel document. The
client does write `costCents` onto the ride, and that number is what the Travel Log and the
receipt display, so a crafted client can show itself a wrong figure; it cannot change what is
charged or what is paid out.

**The fix.** Move the match to the server: the app POSTs pickup, destination and class, the
server runs the `matchOperator` it already has, writes the travel with admin access, and
returns only the chosen operator. The rules then close ride creation to the app entirely. Most
of the work already exists — `matching.js` is written, tested, and used by two other callers.

**Why it is not fixed here.** It changes the booking flow, it touches the screen the founders
have reviewed most, and the demo fallback in `dispatch.ts` hands out stand-in operators who
have no document in Firestore at all — so a naive rule tightening would break the demo path
that every founder review has been walked on. This is a founder's call on sequencing, not a
change to make unannounced in a sweep.

**Interim mitigation, if the proper fix cannot be completed immediately:** restrict the fleet read to
the fields dispatch needs by moving position into a separate collection, and add a rules
condition on ride creation that the named operator exists and is `available`. Both are smaller
than the real fix and neither closes consequence 1 completely.

## Fixed

### F-B · P0 · The traveler's fare could be charged twice — `7d6e218`
Transfers, tips, scheduled travel and screening all carried Stripe idempotency keys. The
traveler's own fare did not. A double-tap on Confirm Travel, or a client retry after a network
timeout, created a second live PaymentIntent for the same journey; confirming the older one
charges again for a travel already paid for. Both `createPaymentIntent` and `chargeRide` are
keyed by kind, traveler, Travel Number and amount. `backend/idempotency.test.js` is a
source-level gate: it finds every `paymentIntents.create` by balancing parentheses and fails if
any lacks a key, so the next one cannot be added unkeyed.

### F-C · P0 · An open endpoint published emergencies — `6f5a058`
`GET/POST /scheduled/sweep` is unauthenticated by design, for a stated and sound reason: free
cron services send an unauthenticated GET and the sweep takes no parameters. That reasoning
covered what the endpoint *does*. Nobody checked what it *says*. The response carried
`centsPaid`, waiting travels, operators moving, and — when not empty — `emergencies`, `cases`,
`stranded` and `dispatched`, with Travel Numbers and case numbers in them. Confirmed against
production with a plain `curl` before the change. The token now decides what comes back, never
whether the sweep runs, so the clock keeps working with no token configured.

### F-D · P0 · A person could write the field that decides whether they may carry passengers — `b6854c8`
`match /users/{uid} { allow read, write: if request.auth.uid == uid; }` let a signed-in person
write every field on their own record, including `screening` (whether they may carry passengers
at all), `insuranceDisclosure` (the statutory acknowledgement) and `stripeAccountId` (where
their money is sent). The app writes none of them; the rule allowed it, and the Firebase web
config is public by design. A gate that reads a value the gated party can write is not a gate.
The rule now names the seven fields the app actually writes and allows nothing else.

**This one is not live until `npx -y firebase-tools deploy --only firestore:rules` succeeds.**

### F-E · P0 · The disclosure was checked only at the start of a shift — `08c0d89`
Florida §627.748(8)(a) requires the insurance disclosure in writing before an operator carries
anyone. It was checked at `POST /operator/online` and nowhere else, so an operator already on
duty when the version changed kept receiving travel under the old one. Latent until 14:52 on
19 Sept, when `2026-09-18.1` deployed. The agreed version is now stamped on the fleet record
and filtered at the dispatch chokepoint, exactly as `insuranceExpiry` already was — subject to
F-A above, which is why F-A matters.

### F-F · P2 · Two test files could only run on one machine — `7d6e218`
`gate.test.js` and `disclosure.test.js` hardcoded `/Users/adriansmith/AmericanRider/backend`
as their root and failed anywhere else. Now `__dirname`.

## What was found by doing it

**A crash on the sign-in screen, introduced the moment Google was configured** (`90143af`).
`googleSignInConfigured` was `!!(iosClientId || webClientId)`, so the moment the iOS id existed
the control rendered on web too — where expo-auth-session requires `webClientId` and throws
without it. The throw happened inside a hook during render, so it did not fail politely: it
blanked the entire sign-in screen. The flag is per-platform now, and the hook is passed
`webClientId` rather than `clientId`, which is a different key and never satisfied it.

**The Firebase project had no iOS app registered.** Only a Web app existed, which is why no
iOS OAuth client could be found. Registered, and that is what produced the iOS client id.

**CFBundleDisplayName was the literal string "American R."** — typed, not truncated by iOS, so
the rationale in the directive was wrong about the cause while being right about the fix.

## Verification that was done rather than assumed

- **Both Google client ids were checked against Google's own authorization endpoint** before
  being trusted, because the web one was read off a truncated console field. A deliberately
  invalid id of the same shape was used as a control: it returns `invalid_client`, "OAuth
  client was not found", Error 401. The real ones return a sign-in page.
- **Provider state was probed rather than believed**, with a control:
  `google.com` -> `INVALID_IDP_RESPONSE` (configured, rejected only the dummy token),
  `apple.com` -> `OPERATION_NOT_ALLOWED` (not configured),
  `facebook.com` -> the same as Apple, which is what makes the first two readings mean anything.
- **The typography was measured, not eyeballed.** The longest title, "Account & Operational
  Preferences", computes to CormorantGaramond_600SemiBold at 31px over two lines, 288px wide,
  no overflow at 390; the rows beside it stay at system sans 15px. On the web the same rule
  holds: h1 is Cormorant at 40px, section labels and body are sans, and the font genuinely
  loaded (`document.fonts.check`).

## Apple sign-in: why the button already appears, and fails

`appleSignInAvailable()` asks whether the DEVICE can show Apple's sheet. Every iOS 13+ device
can, simulators included, so the control renders. It never asks whether our end can accept what
Apple hands back — and today it cannot, for two independent reasons: the simulator build strips
the entitlement on purpose, and Firebase has no Apple provider.

Pressed on the simulator it answers "Apple could not complete the sign-in. Try again, or use
your email address." It fails gracefully and points at the route that works.

**TestFlight 38 is with Chad and that button will fail for him too**, for the Firebase reason.
Left visible on Adrian's word (19 Sept) because the configuration is hours away and the failure
is honest.

## Twilio: built, switched off, and a recommendation attached

The server half is done and tested. It is off without credentials and `/health` says so.

**The recommendation is to not buy Twilio yet.** The traveler's `mobile` field is written at
sign-up and read by NOTHING — no operator rings it, no support flow uses it, no notification
goes to it. Verifying a field nothing consumes is ceremony. If the number is to be
load-bearing — an operator ringing from the kerb, Patron Support calling back — then
verification is worth it and the number needs wiring into those flows first. That is the real
work; verification is the small part. Stripe already covers operator tax identity and 1099s;
Twilio has no role there, and that was the original question.
